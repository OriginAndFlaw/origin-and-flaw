There is a good chance you used it today. A frying pan. Black, smooth, and so slippery that an egg slides across it like ice.

It feels like the most ordinary thing in your kitchen.

But the coating on that pan was born from an accident in a laboratory in nineteen thirty eight. And before it ever touched an egg, it was used to help build the atomic bomb.

This is the story of the slipperiest material on Earth. How it was found by mistake. Why a war needed it. How a fishing trip put it on your stove. And the hidden flaw that took decades to come out.

On the sixth of April, nineteen thirty eight, a young chemist named Roy Plunkett was working for the chemical company DuPont in New Jersey. He was twenty seven years old.

He was not trying to invent a frying pan. He was trying to make a better gas for refrigerators. In the nineteen thirties, fridges were still new, and the chemicals inside them were often toxic. Companies wanted something safe.

Plunkett had made a gas called tetrafluoroethylene, and stored it in small steel cylinders, kept cold.

That morning, his assistant, Jack Rebok, opened the valve of one of those cylinders.

Nothing came out.

No gas. No hiss. Nothing.

That should have meant the cylinder was empty. But it was not. It was still heavy. The scale said the gas had gone into that cylinder, and the gas had not come out.

So where was it?

Plunkett did something most people would not bother to do. He took a saw and cut the cylinder open.

Inside, stuck to the bottom and the walls, was a white, waxy powder.

The gas had not leaked. It had changed. Under the pressure inside the cylinder, the tiny gas molecules had joined together, one after another, into long chains. A gas had turned itself into a solid.

What they had made was polytetrafluoroethylene. Most people just say P T F E.

And it was very, very strange.

Think of a long chain of carbon atoms. Now wrap every single one of them in fluorine atoms, like armour. The bond between carbon and fluorine is one of the strongest bonds in all of chemistry. Very little in the world can break it.

So almost nothing reacts with this material. Strong acids do not eat it. Most solvents do not dissolve it. Water beads up and rolls off.

And it is slippery. Not a little slippery. It has one of the lowest friction values of any solid known. Things slide on it almost as if it were wet ice.

It does not melt until about three hundred and twenty seven degrees Celsius.

DuPont patented it in nineteen forty one.

And then, for a while, nobody knew what to do with it. It was expensive. It was hard to shape. It would not glue to anything, because nothing sticks to it. That was its magic, and its problem.

Then came the war.

In the early nineteen forties, the United States began a secret project to build an atomic bomb. It was called the Manhattan Project.

To make the bomb, they needed a special kind of uranium. Natural uranium is mostly one type of atom, with a tiny amount of a slightly lighter type mixed in. That lighter type was the one they needed.

The two types are almost identical. Separating them is one of the hardest engineering jobs ever attempted.

At Oak Ridge, in Tennessee, they built a gigantic factory to do it. It was called K twenty five. It was one of the largest buildings in the world.

The method was to turn uranium into a gas, uranium hexafluoride, and push it through barriers again and again, so that the slightly lighter atoms slowly pulled ahead.

But uranium hexafluoride is a nightmare. It is one of the most corrosive substances ever handled in industry. It attacks metals. It destroys ordinary seals and gaskets. In a plant with miles of pipes and thousands of valves, every leak was a disaster.

They needed a material that this gas could not touch.

And there was one. The white powder from the cylinder that nobody had wanted.

P T F E was used to coat the valves and seals in the pipes carrying uranium hexafluoride at K twenty five. The material that nothing could stick to was now holding back one of the most aggressive chemicals on Earth.

In nineteen forty five, the war ended with two atomic bombs. And that same year, the material got a brand name.

Teflon.

By nineteen forty eight, DuPont was making more than two million pounds of it every year.

But it still had not reached a kitchen.

In France, an engineer named Marc Grégoire liked to fish. He had used Teflon on his fishing tackle, so his line would not tangle and stick.

His wife, Colette, had a much better idea.

If nothing sticks to it, she said, why not put it on a cooking pan?

In nineteen fifty four, Grégoire found a way to bond the coating to aluminium. Together they started a company. They called it Tefal. Teflon, and aluminium.

In nineteen sixty one, the first non-stick pan made in America went on sale. It was called the Happy Pan.

And from there, it spread to almost every kitchen in the world.

A material born in a refrigerator experiment, and tested in the factory of the atomic bomb, was now frying eggs for breakfast.

But there is a flaw.

Teflon itself is famously inert. That is the whole point of it. The problem was not the coating. It was how the coating was made.

For decades, making it used another chemical, called P F O A, sometimes known as C eight. It helped the process work.

And P F O A did not stay in the factory.

It got into rivers. It got into drinking water. It got into people. Chemicals like this are now often called forever chemicals, because they break down so slowly in nature.

DuPont faced legal action over the health and environmental effects of P F O A, including a large class action from communities near its plant.

In the end, the industry stopped using it. Non-stick cookware has not been made with P F O A since twenty thirteen, and it is no longer produced in the United States.

And something hopeful happened. Between nineteen ninety nine and twenty fourteen, the levels of P F O A found in the blood of Americans fell by about seventy percent.

There is one more thing worth knowing. The coating is safe in normal cooking. But leave an empty pan on high heat, and above about two hundred and sixty degrees Celsius, the coating starts to break down. So do not heat a non-stick pan empty.

So what does this mean for the pan in your kitchen?

The same material is everywhere now. About half of all P T F E goes into wire insulation, for aircraft and computers. It is in waterproof jackets. It is the white tape plumbers wrap around pipe threads. It is in some dental floss. It is in space suits.

And it all started with a cylinder that should have been empty, and a chemist who was curious enough to saw it open.

That is the pattern we see again and again on this channel. The origin of a thing is rarely clean. A breakthrough can be an accident. A kitchen tool can carry the history of a war. And a flaw can hide, not in the product, but in the way it was made.

Next time you slide an egg out of a pan, remember the gas that vanished in nineteen thirty eight.
